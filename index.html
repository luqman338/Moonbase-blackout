    Behind the Mission

    The first version of Moonbase Blackout worked.
    The kids thought it was boring.

    Luqman runs a community program teaching children science, technology, engineering and math (STEM), along with business and leadership. He wanted to make engineering decisions something children could understand and enjoy.

    For NASA Space Apps Challenge 2026, he chose a lunar survival game: one battery, six shifts and three commands. Feedback from children in his program changed how the game tells its story and shows the result of each choice.

    NASA record, clock calculation and scientific limits

    Retrieved observation

    Sources: NASA CCMC SEP record, dated NASA SEP API query and the stored dataset. The SEP object preserves retrieved fields, including linked events, notifications, submission time and version. The wrapper’s provenance fields are local curation metadata.

    Supplied flare start

    The linked flare ID is verified in the SEP data. Independent flare retrieval was unsuccessful. The local flare object is a supplied-fact subset, not a retrieved flare response. For background, see NASA’s explanation of solar flares.

    Mapping into the mission clock

    fictionalStart = suppliedFlareStart − 12 hours
    elapsedMilliseconds = sepObservationTime − fictionalStart
    alertShift = 1 + floor(elapsedMilliseconds / 21600000)

    What the data does not establish

    The blackout, clock offset, 92 kWh battery, command costs and one-shift shelter protection are fictional assumptions. Shelter’s extra 6 kWh represents fictional ventilation demand. Two greenhouse light periods are a game objective, not a crop-growth model.

    The NASA record does not supply those costs or validate the shelter. The model does not calculate lunar radiation dose, shielding, crop physiology, thermal behaviour or real life-support demand. Animated particles are a schematic, not literally visible radiation. No live forecast is used.
